    The telescope

    Work it out●○○ basic3 min

    In the introduction Galileo writes that through the telescope the Moon, nearly sixty Earth semi-diameters away, looks as near as if it were only two of those measures away. By how many times does its diameter appear magnified?

    Expected answer: 30 times

    Solution

    30 times: 60 : 2 = 30. Galileo says so right after: the diameter appears about thirty times larger, the surface about nine hundred.

    Hint

    An object that looks N times nearer looks N times wider.

    For the teacher

    Links apparent distance to angular size: for the same object the angle is inversely proportional to the distance. Students can go on to the surface (900) and the volume (27,000), which Galileo quotes in the same sentence.

    Typical mistakes

    • Answering 58: the difference instead of the ratio.
    • Answering 900, mixing up diameter and surface.

    Goals: Inverse proportionality; angular magnification.

    Try it in the panel●●○ intermediate5 min

    Galileo's first telescope showed objects «one-third of the distance off and nine times larger»; the last one shows them «more than thirty times nearer». Set the last instrument in the panel, then read: by how many times does it magnify surfaces?

    Solution

    900 times (30 × 30): Galileo's «nearly a thousand times». For him «nearer» refers to diameters and «larger» to surfaces: 3 and 9 for the first instrument, 30 and 900 for the last.

    Hint

    For the first instrument «one-third of the distance» and «nine times larger» describe the same thing: 3 × 3 = 9.

    For the teacher

    Galileo measures magnification on surfaces, we measure it on diameters (angular magnification). The question shows that the two numbers describe the same instrument.

    Typical mistakes

    • Thinking that «nearer» and «larger» are two different effects of the instrument.
    • Adding instead of multiplying: 30 + 30 = 60.

    Goals: Scaling of lengths and areas (squared factor).

    Predict, then check●○○ basic4 min

    Before touching the slider. To check an instrument that magnifies surfaces 400 times, Galileo looks at two paper squares, the small one through the telescope and the large one with the naked eye. How many times longer than the small one must the side of the large square be for the two figures to match?

    1. 400 times
    2. 20 times
    3. 200 times
    4. 40 times

    Solution

    20 times: 20² = 400. In the panel the two figures match when the magnification of diameters is 20.

    Hint

    The area of a square grows as the square of its side.

    For the teacher

    «Predict, then check» format: students choose before opening the panel, then check with the slider. It is also an example of measurement by comparison, without graduated instruments.

    Typical mistakes

    • Choosing 400: not distinguishing side and area.
    • Choosing 200: halving instead of taking the square root.

    Goals: Relation between sides and areas of similar figures.

    The face of the Moon

    Find it in the text●○○ basic4 min

    Looking at the Moon four or five days old, Galileo concludes that it is not a perfectly smooth sphere. What would he see along the boundary between light and shadow if it were? What does he see instead? Find the passage with the book's search.

    Solution

    On a smooth sphere the boundary would be a regular line, an ellipse. Galileo sees instead an «irregular, uneven, and very wavy line»: peaks light up before valleys. It is the first clue of mountains and hollows.

    Hint

    Try searching for «spherical» or «ellipse».

    For the teacher

    A good chance to work on the text: ask for the exact words, then why a smooth sphere would give an ellipse (projection of a great circle seen at an angle). The phases panel at figure 1 is useful for comparison.

    Typical mistakes

    • Answering that the boundary «is curved»: it is curved in both cases, the difference is regularity.

    Goals: Reading an observational argument; from phenomenon to cause.

    Open in the bookThe passage in the text

    Predict, then check●●○ intermediate5 min

    Predict, then check from space. At full Moon, where is the Moon?

    1. On the opposite side of the Earth from the Sun
    2. Between the Earth and the Sun
    3. At 90° from the direction of the Sun
    4. Anywhere: the phase depends on how much of the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon

    Solution

    On the opposite side from the Sun: Sun, Earth and Moon are nearly in line with the Earth in the middle, and from here we see the whole lit hemisphere. Between Earth and Sun is new Moon, at 90° the quarters. The Earth's shadow matters only in eclipses.

    Hint

    The Sun always lights half of the Moon. From where can the whole lit half be seen?

    For the teacher

    The last option is the most common misconception about phases. Have the class vote before opening the panel in space and orbiting the view around the Earth.

    Typical mistakes

    • Explaining phases with the Earth's shadow (confusion with eclipses).
    • Thinking that at full Moon the Moon is «close to the Sun» because it is so bright.

    Goals: Sun–Earth–Moon model; geometry of phases.

    The mountains of the Moon

    Choose the answer●○○ basic3 min

    Which observation does Galileo start from to measure the lunar mountains? How far from the boundary of the light does he sometimes see lit peaks?

    1. More than one twentieth of the Moon's diameter
    2. About half the radius
    3. One hundredth of the diameter
    4. Right on the boundary, never far from it

    Solution

    «Sometimes more than one twentieth of the diameter»: 100 miles out of 2000. From this single figure, with Pythagoras' theorem, Galileo derives the height of the mountains.

    Hint

    Search for «twentieth» in the text.

    For the teacher

    Entry question for the mountains panel: it fixes the observed figure before the geometric construction.

    Typical mistakes

    • Choosing «right on the boundary»: that is the case of a smooth Moon, which Galileo is ruling out.

    Goals: Telling the observed data from the model.

    Try it in the panel●●○ intermediate6 min

    How far from the boundary of the light must a peak about 20 miles high be, to be already lit? Find the distance with the panel's slider.

    Solution

    About 200 miles, one tenth of the diameter: DE² = 200² + 1000² = 1,040,000, ED ≈ 1019.8, AD ≈ 19.8 miles. Doubling the distance makes the height about four times larger: h ≈ d²/(2R).

    Hint

    With 100 miles the height is about 5. If you double the distance, does the height double or more?

    For the teacher

    The inverse of Galileo's problem. After the search with the slider, students can solve it algebraically: (R + h)² = R² + d².

    Typical mistakes

    • Expecting linear growth (looking for 400 miles).
    • Confusing the height AD with the length ED.

    Goals: Pythagoras' theorem; quadratic dependence; approximation for d ≪ R.

    Work it out●●○ intermediate4 min

    Galileo puts the Moon at 2000 miles; today we know its diameter is 3474 km. How many kilometres high is the peak in his construction (CD = 100 miles)? Use the panel or calculate.

    Expected answer: 8.7 km (tolerance ± 0.2)

    Solution

    About 8.7 km: 4.99 × 3474 / 2000. Galileo's miles are not needed: what counts is the fraction of the lunar diameter. It is the same order of magnitude as the highest lunar relief, several kilometres high.

    Hint

    What matters is the ratio to the diameter: AD is about 5 parts in 2000.

    For the teacher

    It shows that Galileo's method does not depend on the unit. Link it to the panel's question on Earth's mountains (Everest about 8.8 km).

    Typical mistakes

    • Converting miles to km with some value of the mile (modern or old Italian): the result depends on the mile chosen, while the ratio to the lunar diameter does not depend on the unit.

    Goals: Proportions; dimensionless ratios.

    Earthshine

    Try it in the panel●●○ intermediate5 min

    Galileo writes that the Moon's «secondary brightness» is greater the nearer the Moon is to the Sun. With earthshine switched on, bring the simulation to a day when the dark part is clearly visible.

    Solution

    On the days near new Moon (waxing or waning crescent). Seen from the Moon, the Earth is then nearly «full» and lights it more. After the first quarter earthshine almost disappears, as Galileo writes.

    Hint

    «The nearer the Moon is to the Sun»: in the sky, that is, close to new Moon.

    For the teacher

    The Earth's phases seen from the Moon are complementary to the Moon's phases seen from the Earth. In the space view, point out how much of the lit Earth faces the Moon.

    Typical mistakes

    • Looking for it at full Moon: «more light» does not mean more earthshine.

    Goals: Reflection of light; reasoning from another point of view.

    Choose the answer●●○ intermediate4 min

    Why, according to Galileo, can earthshine not be the Moon's own light or light from the stars?

    1. Because then it would be seen above all in eclipses, when the Moon is in the darkest sky, and it is not
    2. Because the Moon is too far from the stars
    3. Because the telescope does not show it
    4. Because it is red

    Solution

    The Moon's own light (or starlight) would always be there, and would be best seen in eclipses, against the darkest sky. But in eclipses the Moon shows a different light, fainter, reddish and changing. For Galileo the cause is the Earth, reflecting sunlight.

    Hint

    Search for «eclipses» in the paragraph on earthshine.

    For the teacher

    An example of refutation by consequences: if the hypothesis were true we would observe X; we do not. The red colour is a trap: Galileo attributes it to the light seen in eclipses, not to earthshine.

    Typical mistakes

    • Choosing «it is red»: it mixes up the two kinds of light described in the paragraph.

    Goals: Structure of an argument by contradiction.

    Stars, Milky Way and nebulae

    Choose the answer●●○ intermediate4 min

    Through the telescope the Moon is greatly magnified, the stars much less. What is Galileo's explanation?

    1. To the naked eye stars appear fringed with rays that make them look larger; the telescope removes those rays and magnifies only the tiny body
    2. The stars are too far away for the telescope to work
    3. Starlight does not pass well through the lenses
    4. The stars are smaller than the Moon

    Solution

    To the naked eye stars appear «fringed with sparkling rays»: the angle we see is that of the glow around them, not of the star itself. The telescope removes this glow and then magnifies a very small body.

    Hint

    Galileo recalls that at twilight even the brightest stars look tiny.

    For the teacher

    We now know that a star's disc is below the resolving power of any telescope: what we see is a diffraction and turbulence pattern. Galileo's insight (apparent size is not real size) is right.

    Typical mistakes

    • Choosing «too far away»: the Moon is far too, yet it is magnified.

    Goals: Apparent and real size; the eye's role in measurement.

    Work it out●○○ basic2 min

    How many stars does Galileo draw in Praesepe, besides the Aselli? Find the number in the text.

    Expected answer: 36 stars

    Solution

    Thirty-six. To the naked eye Praesepe looks like a single bright patch: Galileo shows it is a cluster of stars.

    Hint

    Search for «Praesepe».

    For the teacher

    Today Praesepe is the open cluster M44, with hundreds of stars about 600 light-years away. Compare it with Orion's Head, where Galileo counts 21 stars.

    Typical mistakes

    • Answering «more than forty»: that is the overall estimate, not the stars drawn.

    Goals: Careful reading of a numerical datum.

    The Medicean Stars

    Try it in the panel●●○ intermediate6 min

    On the first evening, 7 January, Galileo sees three little stars: two to the east and one to the west. The model computes four satellites, all outside Jupiter's disc. Open the first drawing and show the names: why does Galileo see only three?

    Solution

    Io and Europa are both about 2.3′ east of Jupiter, less than a tenth of a minute apart: in Galileo's telescope they are a single «star». Callisto is about 9′ east, Ganymede about 5.5′ west.

    Hint

    Look at the distances in minutes of arc: two satellites are almost at the same spot.

    For the teacher

    Introduces resolving power: two sources closer than a certain angle cannot be told apart. It is also a good example of a modern model helping to read a historical datum.

    Typical mistakes

    • Thinking the missing satellite is behind Jupiter: in the model they are all outside the disc.

    Goals: Angles in arcminutes; resolving power; data–model comparison.

    Try it in the panel●●● challenging8 min

    Use the free-time slider (or the graph) to find a moment, between January and March 1610, when all four satellites are visible on the same side of Jupiter.

    Solution

    It happens only a few times in two months, for a few hours: for example on 14–15 January, 24 January or 19 February. The time–elongation graph is the right tool to find them.

    Hint

    In the graph look for a horizontal line where the four curves are all on the same side of Jupiter's band.

    For the teacher

    An open exploration question. Reading the graph (time downwards, elongation horizontally) is the key skill: each of Galileo's drawings is a horizontal slice of it.

    Typical mistakes

    • Counting satellites hidden behind or in front of Jupiter.
    • Mixing up east and west: in the drawings east is on the left.

    Goals: Reading graphs; superposed periodic motions.

    The conclusions on Jupiter

    Find it in the text●●○ intermediate4 min

    From which observation does Galileo infer that the satellites with the smallest orbits move fastest? Find the passage.

    Solution

    The satellites nearest Jupiter are often seen in the east when the day before they were in the west, and vice versa: they change sides quickly. The outermost one instead has a period of about half a month.

    Hint

    Search for «smallest circles».

    For the teacher

    Link to Kepler's third law (modern reading in the Medicean Stars panel): r³/T² is about the same for the four satellites.

    Typical mistakes

    • Answering with Kepler's law: the question asks what Galileo observes, not today's explanation.

    Goals: From qualitative data to a law; period and radius.

    Choose the answer●●○ intermediate4 min

    Galileo says he has an «notable and splendid argument» for those hesitant to accept Copernicus. Which objection do Jupiter's satellites remove?

    1. That the Earth could not go round the Sun carrying the Moon with it
    2. That the fixed stars should show a parallax
    3. That a stone dropped from a tower should be left behind
    4. That Venus shows no phases

    Solution

    Opponents of Copernicus objected that the Earth cannot go round the Sun dragging the Moon along. Jupiter shows four moons following it as it moves: such a system really exists.

    Hint

    Reread the sentence about «the motion of one Moon about the Earth».

    For the teacher

    The other three options are real objections to the Copernican system, dealt with elsewhere (parallax, the tower in the Dialogue, the phases of Venus observed in 1610 after the Sidereus). Ask students to place them.

    Typical mistakes

    • Thinking the Sidereus proves the Copernican system: it removes an objection, it does not prove it.

    Goals: Distinguishing proof from removing an objection.
